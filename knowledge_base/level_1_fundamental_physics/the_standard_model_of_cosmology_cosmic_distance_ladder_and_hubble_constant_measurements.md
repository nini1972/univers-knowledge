# The Standard Model of Cosmology: Cosmic Distance Ladder and Hubble Constant Measurements

## 1. Overview
The cosmic distance ladder is a sequence of observational methods used to establish distances to increasingly remote astronomical objects and determine the local value of the Hubble constant, \(H_0\). Its methodology is empirically grounded. Independent studies also support the existence of a discrepancy between local \(H_0\) measurements and the value inferred from Planck cosmic microwave background observations under the ΛCDM model.

The observational discrepancy is established; proposed physical changes to resolve it remain theoretical and have not been verified.

## 2. Detailed Explanation
The local distance ladder links measurements through overlapping steps. Geometric distance anchors calibrate nearby standard candles, including Cepheid variable stars. Those calibrations are then used to determine distances to Type Ia supernovae (SNe Ia), which extend the measurement to more distant galaxies. At sufficiently low redshift, the relation between recession velocity and distance can be used to infer \(H_0\).

The Planck result follows a different route: it measures features of the cosmic microwave background and infers \(H_0\) within the assumptions of flat ΛCDM. It is therefore a model-dependent inference, unlike the local distance-ladder determination.

Independent approaches provide additional comparisons. The Tip of the Red Giant Branch (TRGB) method uses a separate stellar population as a distance indicator, while strong-lensing time delays provide another route to estimating \(H_0\). These studies support the empirical observation that local measurements and the Planck ΛCDM inference do not agree. They do not, by themselves, establish which measurement or physical assumptions account for the discrepancy.

## 3. Mathematical Framework
The distance modulus connects apparent magnitude \(m\), absolute magnitude \(M\), and distance \(d\):

\[
\mu = m - M = 5\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{d}{10\ {\rm pc}}\right).
\]

Cepheid variable stars are calibrated using the Leavitt law, which relates their period \(P\) to absolute magnitude:

\[
M_{\rm Cep} = a\log_{10}P + b.
\]

At low redshift, the approximate relation between recession velocity and distance is:

\[
cz \approx H_0 d.
\]

The CMB inference of \(H_0\) depends on the assumed cosmological model. In ΛCDM, the sound horizon and expansion history are related by expressions including:

\[
r_s = \int_{z_*}^{\infty} \frac{c_s(z)}{H(z)}\,dz.
\]

These relations describe how the distinct observational routes connect measured quantities to distance and expansion rate. The local and CMB-based determinations rely on different methods and assumptions.

## 4. Skeptical Perspectives & Alternative Hypotheses
The distance ladder’s calibration steps can be affected by observational and calibration systematics. The TRGB determination provides an independent calibration route and a comparison with Cepheid-based results. Differences between methods warrant continued testing rather than an assumption that a single measurement settles the issue.

Proposed physical resolutions—including changes to early-Universe or late-time cosmological physics—remain theoretical. Reviews of possible solutions catalog many proposals, but their existence does not constitute empirical verification of any particular resolution (Di Valentino et al. 2021). The approved conclusion is therefore limited: the methodology and discrepancy are empirically grounded, while proposed physical resolutions are not verified.

## 5. Verification & Skeptic's Notes
The cosmic distance-ladder methodology and the observational discrepancy between local Hubble-constant measurements and the Planck ΛCDM inference are supported by multiple independent studies. The studies use different observational routes, including Cepheid-calibrated supernovae, TRGB calibration, CMB observations interpreted under ΛCDM, and strong-lensing time delays.

These independent measurements substantiate the existence of the discrepancy, but do not independently verify a proposed new-physics explanation. The physical resolutions discussed in the literature should be treated as hypotheses, not established results.
